Which Pages Should an Editor Review First? Ranking Search-Visibility Drops by Risk and Stake

A rolling-origin, client-grouped evaluation of a decision-support review queue, with one sealed month.

Muhammad Mohid Shafiq · October 2026 · Repository · Capstone notebook

Abstract

Content teams can review only about fifty pages every two weeks, out of tens of thousands that earn search impressions, so the useful question is which pages losing visibility deserve that attention first. On a pseudonymized warehouse of 78.8 million daily Search Console rows (FlyRank ML Internship release), we score 659,482 page-dates from 51 sites at ten biweekly decision dates (January–May 2026) and seal June 2026 for one final test. We compare a transparent rule (rank by impressions lost last week) with learned rankers under a rolling-origin, client-grouped design (train only on earlier dates and other sites) and score every 50-page queue on precision and on the share of achievable lost visibility it covers. A learned probability alone reaches the highest precision (0.81) but covers only 8% of the visibility at stake, while weighting the rule's lost impressions by that probability raises precision@50 from 0.64 to 0.69 (+0.054, 95% interval +0.030 to +0.079) at equal visibility (0.41 vs 0.42); in sealed June the precision lead held (0.85 vs 0.81, higher in 9 of 10 queues) while visibility came in slightly below the rule (0.54 vs 0.56). The output is a decision-support review queue with action routes, reason codes, calibrated confidence and drift triggers: it ranks human attention and makes no causal claim about refresh impact.

1 · Introduction and problem statement

Every two weeks a content editor has time for about fifty page reviews. The portfolio behind this study offers 42,331 to 82,673 pages with real search exposure per cycle, so a review queue spends roughly one slot per thousand pages. The decision this work supports is the order of that queue: which pages, losing search visibility right now, deserve a human look first.

The obvious answer is a rule: review the pages that lost the most impressions last week. It is transparent and decent. The obvious upgrade is a model that predicts which pages will keep declining. Our first model did exactly that: 0.96 precision@50 on one held-out split. It was also nearly useless, because its fifty picks were tiny pages whose collapse no one would spend an hour on. That tension shapes this paper: a review queue has to be right (precision) and it has to matter (visibility at stake).

Claims.

  1. C1. Weighting the rule's lost impressions by a learned probability that the drop persists beats the frozen rule on precision@50 by +0.054 (95% interval +0.030 to +0.079) without a measurable loss of visibility, across 40 rolling-origin, client-grouped queues (§4.1–4.2).
  2. C2. Precision alone picks the wrong system. Probability-only rankers, and a no-training sort on the same signal, reach 0.81–0.83 precision but cover 8–9% of the achievable lost visibility, and extra model complexity buys nothing measurable (§4.1).
  3. C3. The evaluation boundary is load-bearing. The Week-5 model falls from 0.96 on one split to 0.85 ± 0.14 under the honest design, and a model handed client and date codes gains +0.21 precision from a careless split (§4.4).
  4. C4. In a sealed month the precision lead replicated, but the visibility guardrail slipped and the label regime moved beyond anything seen in development. Monitoring has to read lift over a moving base rate (§4.5, §6).

3 · Methodology

3.1 Unit, label and assumptions

One observation is one page at one decision date T. Features use only the 14 days before T, split into week 1 and week 2; the label uses only the 14 days from T. The label declined_robust is true when impressions over the outcome window fall more than 20% below the feature window, after removing each window's single biggest day. An earlier review found one-day spikes fooling both the rule and a plain sum label. The plain contract label is reported as a robustness check. We assume an editor capacity of 50 pages per cycle and judge pages against their own recent level.

Three further pre-T candidates (four-week trend, peak-day share, site-wide slide) were tested and did not earn a place (§4.1).

3.3 Baseline and policy

Baseline: the frozen rule. Flag a page when its week-2 impressions are more than 20% below week 1, and rank flagged pages by impressions lost (week 1 − week 2). It was frozen in Week 4, before any model existed. One property matters: if a page simply stays at its week-2 level, its two-week loss is exactly that number, so the rule is a persistence forecast of the loss.

Policy. score = P(decline) × lost weekly impressions, where P comes from logistic regression on the five features. The rule supplies the stake and the model supplies the chance that the drop is real and lasting. A probability alone ranks tiny, fully collapsed pages first; the multiplication fixes that.

Selection rule, declared in advance. Precision@50 is the primary metric, fixed in Week 2. Visibility@50 (the share of the 50 largest achievable losses the queue actually covers) is a guardrail. It was added after the Week-5 error analysis and before the sealed test. A candidate must beat the rule on precision without losing visibility, and among those that pass the simplest wins.

3.4 Validation design

Rolling origin × client groups. Clients are split into five fixed folds. For each of the eight test dates from 2026-02-02 to 2026-05-11, every model is trained only on earlier dates and on clients in other folds, then builds one 50-page queue per held-out fold: 8 × 5 = 40 queues, each scored against its own base rate. Paired differences carry 95% intervals from a bootstrap that resamples whole dates, because the five folds of one date share a market week. The sealed month was read exactly once, by one notebook cell, after everything was frozen. That cell fingerprints the frozen setup (d9e8d9bfbd813d89) and fails if anything changes afterwards.

3.5 Leakage controls

  • Windows are tested in code: 21 unit tests on synthetic data cover window boundaries, IS TRUE handling, both labels, eligibility, the rule's order, split guards and the metric maths.
  • The shared module refuses any outcome-window column as a feature. With that guard bypassed on purpose, a planted leak lifts AUC from 0.614 to 0.994.
  • Shuffled labels fall to chance (AUC 0.499).
  • No single feature is suspiciously strong (single-feature AUCs 0.33–0.53).
  • Product decision flags do not exist in the release.

4.2 Uncertainty

Policy minus rule, paired by queue, with 95% intervals from the whole-date bootstrap:

  • precision@50: +0.054 [+0.030, +0.079]
  • visibility@50: +0.009 [-0.031, +0.056]
  • AUC: +0.042 [+0.026, +0.059]

The precision interval excludes zero. The visibility interval straddles it: no measurable loss, which is exactly what the guardrail asks. The policy matches or beats the rule on precision in 34 of 40 queues and on all 8 test dates, but on visibility in only 16 of 40. On visibility it is a tie, not a win. On the contract label the picture holds: precision 0.722 vs 0.663, visibility 0.475 vs 0.463.

5 · Limitations and honest framing

  • A short history. Eight development dates and two sealed dates, with the base rate moving from 0.14 to 0.59. These results describe this portfolio in this period. A longer, multi-season backtest is the most valuable missing experiment.
  • High variance. The policy's precision@50 ranges widely across queues (SD 0.17). The mean advantage is real; any single cycle can go either way.
  • The label partly measures the market. A fixed 20% threshold counts site-wide and portfolio-wide swings as page declines. On 2026-04-27 the whole portfolio lost 18% of its impressions. Routes and triggers mitigate this; a site-relative label is the natural next step.
  • Persistence, not early warning. The strongest signal is a drop already visible at T. On pages asked to fall further below their week-2 level, the slide signal falls to chance. The queue triages visible drops; it does not foresee new ones.
  • The visibility guardrail slipped in June (0.544 vs 0.560).
  • Coverage. Search Console only, and pages above the floor only: about 21% of tracked pages, holding 97% of impressions. Absence from the queue is not evidence of health. There are no click, engagement or conversion outcomes.
  • Data quality. About 1% of sizeable queue rows share an identical Search Console series with another record. A small share of rows report an impossible position below 1. The content dates are export snapshots.
  • Observational, not causal. Every number here is observed or measured on one pseudonymized portfolio. Nothing shows that reviewing or refreshing a flagged page changes its outcome; that would need an experiment, such as randomising which queued pages are reviewed first. The system is decision-support: it orders human attention.

6 · Ranked recommendations: the action playbook

Applied to the latest date the release supports, 2026-06-22 (outcome not yet in the data), the frozen system scored 76,094 pages; the 46,709 that lost impressions before that date entered the queue. Both pre-release triggers were quiet (feature drift PSI 0.05; site-level share of the top 50 0.24), so the queue would be released. Its top 50 hold 30 refresh reviews, 12 site-level checks, 4 technical checks and 4 pages to monitor.

  1. Work the refresh reviews in score order. Each is a large page whose loss is likely to persist. The confidence band tells you how likely: in the backtest, high-band picks persisted 83% of the time, medium 64%, low 46%.
  2. Send site-level rows to whoever owns the site, before touching pages. When a whole site slides, a page rewrite is the wrong first move.
  3. Run a technical check on collapses (indexing, redirects, canonicals). A page that falls to near zero in a week usually has a visibility problem, not a content problem.
  4. Hold spike and new-page rows for one cycle. The largest page in this queue is routed to monitoring: one day carried its whole window.
  5. Dedupe before review, and never automate an edit. Every row is a human decision. Nothing here deletes, redirects or rewrites anything on its own.

Monitoring and retraining

Refit at every decision date on all labelled history, exactly as the backtest does. Two checks run before release: T1, feature drift against the previous cycle (PSI > 0.25), and T2, more than half the top 50 routed to site-level checks. Two run after labels arrive: T3, a base-rate move > 0.10, which only annotates the cycle; and T4, the model losing to the rule two cycles in a row, which means falling back to the rule. Replayed over development, the stop-level checks fired only at the portfolio event of 2026-04-27 and the cycle after it, and they caught it from week-2 data before any label existed.
